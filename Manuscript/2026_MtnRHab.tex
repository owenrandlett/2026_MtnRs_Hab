\begin{abstract}

Melatonin is a circadianly-regulated hormone produced by the pineal gland and retina, with roles in sleep and circadian rhythms as well as other neuronal processes such as neuronal plasticity and learning.
Melatonin signals mostly via the G-protein coupled receptors MT1 (Mtnr1a) and MT2 (Mtnr1b), which show distinct expression patterns and functional roles.
Using larval zebrafish, we previously identified Melatonin as a potent modulator of habituation learning and -- the progressive suppression of responses to a repeated stimulus.
Here we have extended these analyses and have found that Melatonin has strong bidirectional effects on habituation: it potentiates habituation of response probability and latency, while actually inhibiting habituation of movement amplitude and duration. 
Through systematic CRISPR mutagenesis of all six zebrafish Melatonin receptors, we found that only two MT1-type receptors (Mtnr1aa and Mtnr1al) are required, while the MT2-type receptors and Mtnr1c are dispensable for Melatonin's effects on habituation.
Individual \emph{mtnr1aa} and \emph{mtnr1al} mutants show partial insensitivity to Melatonin, while \emph{mtnr1aa;mtnr1al} double mutants show complete insensitivity, demonstrating they act in a cooperative and non-redundant manner.
Finally, expression pattern analyeses confirm that \emph{mtnr1aa} is more widely and highly expressed than \emph{mtnr1al}, and functional analyses using whole-brain activity mapping indicate that Mtnr1aa acts primarily the optic tectum and other visual areas, while Mtnr1al exerts its effects most prominently in the preoptic area.
Together, these results show that Melatonin promotes habituation learning through the independent action of two MT1-type receptors, which act in different brain regions. 
These findings provide a molecular and circuit-level dissection of how Melatonin modulates a fundamental form of learning, revealing a functional subdivision between closely related receptor paralogs.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Habituation is an evolutionarily conserved forms of learning, defined as a progressive and reversible decrease in responsiveness to a repeatedly presented stimulus \citep{Rankin2009-no}.
First systematically characterized by Thompson and Spencer, habituation displays a well-defined set of parametric properties: it is stimulus-specific, recovers spontaneously after a rest period, is accelerated by high-frequency stimulus presentation, and can be disrupted by the introduction of a novel stimulus \citep{Thompson1966}.
Far from being a passive consequence of neural fatigue or sensory adaptation, habituation is now recognized as an active form of synaptic plasticity \citep{McDiarmid2019-td}, involving persistent changes at multiple synapses within sensory-motor circuits \citep{Randlett2019-fj, ramaswami2014network, Cooke2020, Nelson2022}.
Deficits in habituation are associated with neurodevelopmental and psychiatric conditions including autism spectrum disorder, schizophrenia, and ADHD \citep{McDiarmid2017}, underscoring its relevance to normal cognitive function and its potential as a tractable behavioral readout of circuit-level plasticity.

Larval zebrafish have emerged as a powerful system for dissecting the neural and molecular substrates of habituation learning.
When exposed to a sudden transition to whole-field darkness (a dark flash, DF), larvae execute a stereotyped escape maneuver called the O-bend \citep{Burgess2007-2}, which habituates robustly with repeated stimulation \citep{wolman_chemical_2011, Randlett2019-fj}.
The transparency and genetic tractability of larvae, combined with whole-brain activity mapping and calcium imaging approaches, have enabled identification of distributed plasticity across multiple brain regions that collectively drive DF habituation learning \citep{Randlett2019-fj, Lamire2023-he}. 
Furthermore, the accessibility of larval zebrafish to high-throughput pharmacological and genetic screening has allowed systematic identification of neuromodulatory pathways that regulate habituation performance \citep{wolman_chemical_2011, Wolman2015}.
Through such a screen, we previously identified Melatonin as one of the most potent modulators of dark-flash habituation learning, with acute exposure causing a dramatic enhancement of habituation for the probability of executing an O-bend response \citep{Lamire2023-he}.

Melatonin is a neuroendocrine hormone synthesized from tryptophan via serotonin, with the rate-limiting step catalyzed by arylalkylamine N-acetyltransferase (AANAT) \citep{Ganguly2002}.
Its production is under tight circadian control, driven by the sympathetic innervation of the pineal gland and suppressed by light, resulting in high nocturnal concentrations in the blood and cerebrospinal fluid across vertebrate species, including zebrafish \citep{Falcn2009, Cahill1996, Gandhi2015_NEURON}.
In zebrafish, two AANAT paralogs contribute to Melatonin synthesis: Aanat1, expressed primarily in the retina, and Aanat2, expressed in both the retina and the pineal gland \citep{Gandhi2015_NEURON, Hill2026}.
This positions Melatonin as a key hormonal signal coupling the external light/dark cycle to internal physiological state.

Melatonin acts primarily through two G-protein coupled receptors, MT1 (Mtnr1a) and MT2 (Mtnr1b), which signal through Gi proteins to reduce intracellular cAMP, though they exhibit distinct binding kinetics, signaling properties, and expression patterns \citep{Dubocovich2005, Liu2016, Cecon2017}.
Both receptors are broadly expressed in the brain, including regions implicated in circadian regulation, sleep, and plasticity \citep{Ng2017, Klosen2019}.
Zebrafish possess an expanded repertoire of six Melatonin receptors: three MT1-type (Mtnr1aa, Mtnr1ab, Mtnr1al), two MT2-type (Mtnr1ba, Mtnr1bb), and Mtnr1c, an ortholog of the mammalian Melatonin-insensitive GPR50 \citep{Hill2026}.
This expanded repertoire provides a unique genetic handle for dissecting the functional contributions of individual receptor subtypes \emph{in vivo}.

Beyond its canonical roles in sleep and circadian entrainment, Melatonin has been increasingly implicated in synaptic plasticity and cognitive function.
In rodents, MT1/MT2 signaling shapes time-of-day-dependent hippocampal synaptic plasticity and learning efficiency \citep{Jilg2019-oy}, and genetic deletion of both receptors alters cognitive and motor performance \citep{ONealMoffitt2014}.
Individual receptor knockouts reveal distinct functional roles: MT2-deficient mice show impaired hippocampal long-term potentiation \citep{Larson2006} and reduced long-term recognition memory \citep{Pistono2021}, while deletion of either MT1 or MT2 alone is sufficient to abrogate reward-associated conditioned place preference \citep{Clough2014}.
Melatonin more broadly supports neuroplasticity through modulation of cAMP/CREB signaling \citep{ValdsTovar2018}.
These findings argue that Melatonin receptor signaling has important functions in learning that are separable from its role in sleep promotion, but the circuit mechanisms and specific receptor subtypes responsible have not been well defined.

Here we leverage the zebrafish system to address this gap.
Using a large behavioral dataset, we first show that Melatonin has complex, bidirectional effects on multiple independently regulated components of dark-flash habituation, potentiating learning for response probability and latency while inhibiting habituation of movement amplitude and duration.
We then demonstrate through systematic CRISPR mutagenesis of all six zebrafish Melatonin receptors that two MT1-type receptors, Mtnr1aa and Mtnr1al, cooperatively and non-redundantly mediate these effects.
Finally, using whole-brain pERK activity mapping, we show that these two receptors appear to act in largely non-overlapping neural circuits.
Together, these data provide a molecular and circuit-level dissection of how Melatonin modulates a fundamental form of learning through the independent action of receptor paralogs in distinct brain regions.

Through a high-throughput drug screen, we previously identified Melatonin as a potent modulator of habituation learning in larval zebrafish \citep{Lamire2023-he}.
Based on this previous dataset, we concluded that Melatonin potently enhances habituation learning for the probability of executing an O-bend response to repeated dark-flash stimuli. 
Importantly, we found that Melatonin had little effect on the habituation of the magnitude of the O-bend response, which is governed by a distinct plasticity mechanisms \citep{Randlett2019-fj}, and that Melatonin did not affect the response of the larvae to acoutsic or optomotor stimuli \citep{Lamire2023-he}. 
This demonstrates that Melatonin does not have a generalized effect on sensory processing or motor output (e.g. by simply inducing sleep \citealp{Gandhi2015_NEURON, Hill2026}). 

We have extended these analyses using a larger dataset, and confirmed that acute exposure to \SI{1}{\micro\Molar} Melatonin potently enhances habituation learning for the Probability of Response (\autoref{fig:1}A). The response to the first 3 dark-flash stimuli are not dramatically altered by the treatment ("Naive Response": \autoref{fig:1}Aii), but the effect is pronounced during the remaining 57 flashes of the first training block ("Block 1" \autoref{fig:1}Aii), the 180 flashes in the subsequent 3 training blocks ("Trained Response": \autoref{fig:1}Aiii), and the re-test block 5 hours after the last training session ("Block 5": \autoref{fig:1}Avi). Consistent with our previous data, the response of the larvae to the acoustic stimuli was indistinguishable from controls ("Acoustic Response": \autoref{fig:1}Ai,v, Bi,v ; \citealp{Lamire2023-he}), confirming that effect of Melatonin is not one of generalized sedation. 
From these data we can conclude that Melatonin potentiates habituation learning for the Probability component of behaviour. 

This larger dataset now also allowed us to more carefully examine the effects of Melatonin on the behavioural components related to the magnitude of the O-bend response. Indeed, a significant enhancement of habituation was also observed for the response Latency, which increases by ~50-100 msec over habituation training (\autoref{fig:1}B). 
This effect was also visible in our original datasets \citep{Lamire2023-he}, but was not previously clear and significant, likely due to the higher degree of variability in these kinematic behavioural measure relative to the Probability component, as well as the fact that the strong suppression of responses by Melatonin results in little data for the kinematic measures, which can only be observed when fish respond to the stimuli. 

Interestingly, while the habituation of the Latency component was potentiated by Melatonin, the opposite was observed for Bend Amplitude (the maximum tail-angle bending achieved during the O-bend movement) (\autoref{fig:1}C), as well as the Movement Duration (the time taken from the intiation of the movement until its termination) (\autoref{fig:1}D). 
Both of these measures normally decrease with training, resulting in movements with reduced amplitudes, and this effect was significantly inhibited by Melatonin, with the response of the Melatonin-treated larvae showing a much weaker modulation of these components during habituation training. 
As with Probability and Latency, the naive response is much less affected than the response seen during training, indicating that the primary effect is on the modulation of the response rather than their innate sensitivity.


Finally, while Melatonin inhibits habituation of Bend Amplitude and Movement Duration, we did not observe any substantial changes when analyzing the habituation performance curves for Displacement (the distance traveled by the larvae during the O-bend movement) (\autoref{fig:1}E) or Reorientation (the change in heading direction achieved by the larvae during the O-bend movement) (\autoref{fig:1}F), indicating that Melatonin does not have a substantial effect on these aspects of habituation.
However, in these two components, the response is subtly but consistently increased in the Melatonin-treated group, primarily in the Naive and Block 1 response, indicating that Melatonin has a weak stimulatory effect on these aspects of the movements, but does not have a substantial effect on the overall learning process.

Collectively, these analyses of habituation learning reveal that Melatonin has a complex and bidirectional effect on different components of habituation learning, potentiating some aspects of learning while inhibiting others, and leaving others unaffected. 
This is consistent with a model in which Melatonin acts primarily in this context on specific neural circuits that govern distinct components of behaviour and habituation learning, rather than having a generalized effect, like modulating sleep or arousal state.

Our pharmacological experiments have shown that exogenously applied Melatonin has a potent effect on habituation learning, but the relevance of endogenous Melatonin on learning is not clear. To address this question, we tested zebrafish mutants that are unable to produce Melatonin due to mutations in the Arylalkylamine N-acetyltransferase 1 (Aanat1) and Arylalkylamine N-acetyltransferase 2 (Aanat2) enzymes, which are expressed in the retina, and the retina and pineal gland, respectively \citep{Gandhi2015_NEURON, Hill2026}. If endogenously produced Melatonin influences habituation in the same ways as exogenous Melatonin, we would expect these Melatonin-deficient mutants to exhibit learning deficits for Probability and Latency, and increased learning performance for Bend Amplitude and Movement Duration. Consistent with this hypothesis, we found that  double mutants (\emph{aanat1\textsubscript{-/-}}; \emph{aanat2\textsubscript{-/-}}) show a significant impairment in habituation learning for the Probability of response to repeated dark-flash stimuli (\autoref{fig:2}A). Similarly to the effect of exogenous Melatonin, this effect is most pronounced during the later stages of training, and is not significant for the naive response to the first 3 dark-flash stimuli. Notably, a  effect was also only observed in the double mutants, indicating both Aanat1 and Aanat2 contribute to the production of Melatonin that promotes habituation learning, and that there is some degree of functional redundancy between these enzymes.

Contrary to our expectations, habituation-dependent modulation of the Latency component of the O-bend response, as well as the other kinmatic parameters, were not strongly affected in the double mutants (\autoref{fig:2}B-F). However, movement Duration did show a significantly more strongly suppressed response in double mutants, consistent with a role for Melatonin in inhibiting this learning effect (\autoref{fig:2}C-F).

These experiments demonstrate that endogenous Melatonin production is required for normal habituation, but this effect is only strong and robust for the Probability of response. 
This is consistent with a model in which Melatonin most strongly affects this aspect of learning. 
However, these experiments were performed beginning in the morning, when Melatonin production is naturally low, and therefore the effect of endogenous Melatonin on learning may be artificially limited in this context.
Also, Melatonin synthesis is suppressed by light. 
Since our Dark Flash stimulus paradigm requires the animals to be light-adapted, little endogenous Melatonin is expected to be produced during these experiments. 
To try and overcome these limitations, we also performed experiments in which the larvae were raised in a shifted light cycle, such that they were tested during the circadian night when Melatonin production is naturally high. 
In these experiments, we observed a similar pattern of results, with the only robust effect again observed for the Probability of response (\autoref{fig:2}G), and no significant effect on Latency (\autoref{fig:2}H) or the kinematic parameters (\autoref{fig:2}I-L). 
From these data we can conclude that endogenous Melatonin production is required for normal habituation learning for the Probability of response, but that its role in regulating the other learning parameters is unclear.
This is likely due to the limitations of our visual assay, which are not ideal for testing the role of endogenous Melatonin as they require light-adaptation which is known to suppress Melatonin production.
Therefore, for the remainder of the experiments we have focused on the strong and robust effect of exogenous Melatonin on habituation learning in order to test the genetic and circuit roles of Melatonin receptors (Mtnrs).

In mammals, Melatonin signals through two G-protein coupled receptors, MT1 (Mtnr1a) and MT2 (Mtnr1b), which are expressed in the brain and other tissues.
Zebrafish have 6 identified MtnRs, including 3 MT1-type receptors (Mtnr1aa, Mtnr1ab, Mtnr1al), 2 MT2-type receptors (Mtnr1ba, Mtnr1bb), and the additional Mtnr1c, which is homologous to the Melatonin-insensitive GPR50 receptor in mammals \citep{Hill2026}. '
To identify which of these receptors mediate the effects of Melatonin on habituation learning, we generated CRISPR mutants for each of the 6 Mtnrs, and tested their effect on the modulation of habituation learning by exogenous Melatonin, to determine if we could identify mutants that are insensitive to the effects of Melatonin on habituation. 
Since the endogenous effects of Melatonin are subtle (\autoref{fig:2}), we focused our analyses of mutants on the effects of endogenous Melatonin, reasoning that mutants for the relevant receptor(s) should show a significant change in habituation relative to their sibling controls in the presence of Melatonin, behaving more similarly to untreated larvae. 

\subsubsection{Mtnr1aa and Mtnr1al are each partially required for the effects of Melatonin on habituation learning}

Mtnr1aa is the most broadly expressed MtnR in the zebrafish brain, and is expressed in many brain regions that are implicated in habituation learning, including the forebrain, midbrain and hindbrain \citep{Hill2026}, making them the strongest candidate for mediating the effects of Melatonin on habituation learning. Consistent with this hypothesis, we found that \emph{mtnr1aa} mutants exibit reduced responsiveness to  the effect of Melatonin on habituation learning for the Probability of response (\autoref{fig:3}A). The effects were less clear for Latency (\autoref{fig:3}B), and the kinemtaic components of the response  (\autoref{fig:3}D-F), but we consistently observed a trend towards less Melatonin response, but this did not always reach statistical significance. From these results we conclude that Mtnr1aa does mediate the effects of Melatonin on habituation learning, but that other receptors likely also contribute to this effect as some Melatonin sensitivity remains. 

Mtnr1ab is the most closely related paralog to Mtnr1aa, and is expressed in a more restricted pattern in the brain, including in the forebrain and midbrain, but not the hindbrain \citep{Hill2026}. We found no evidence that \emph{mtnr1ab} mutants show deficits in the effects of Melatonin on habituation learning (\autoref{fig:3}G-L), indicating that this receptor does not play a major role in mediating the effects of Melatonin on habituation learning. Therefore, we turned our attention to the other MT1-type receptor, Mtnr1al, whose expression pattern is less clear as the gene appears to be very weakly expressed \citep{Hill2026}. Desipite this restricted pattern, We found that \emph{mtnr1al} mutants also show a significant reduction in the effects of Melatonin on habituation learning for the Probability of response (\autoref{fig:3}M), and a similar trend for the other behavioural components (\autoref{fig:3}N-R). From these data we can conclude that Mtnr1al also contributes to mediating the effects of Melatonin on habituation learning.

Having identified Mtnr1aa and Mtnr1al as two receptors that contribute to mediating the effects of Melatonin on habituation learning, we next wanted to determine if the MT2-type receptors (Mtnr1ba and Mtnr1bb) or Mtnr1c, may also modulate habituation learning. Each of these receptors are expressed in the brain, but in more restricted patterns than \emph{mtnr1aa} \citep{Hill2026}. As with the other receptors, we generated CRISPR mutants for each of these receptors, and tested their effect on the modulation of habituation learning by exogenous Melatonin. As with \emph{mtnr1ab}, we found no evidence that mutants for either of the MT2-type receptors, Mtnr1ba and Mtnr1bb, or Mtnr1c show deficits in the effects of Melatonin on habituation learning (\autoref{fig:4}A-L), indicating that these receptors do not play a major role in mediating the effects of Melatonin on habituation learning.

Our experiments with the individual mutants found that both Mtnr1aa and Mtnr1al contribute to mediating the effects of Melatonin on habituation learning, but that each contributes only partially, approximately half of the effect. 
Therefore, we wanted to determine if these receptors act in a cooperative manner to mediate these effects. 
To test this hypothesis, we generated double mutants for \emph{mtnr1aa} and \emph{mtnr1al}, and tested their effect on the modulation of habituation learning by exogenous Melatonin. 
We found that the double mutants show a much more robust reduction in the effects of Melatonin on habituation learning for the Probability of response (\autoref{fig:5}A), where double mutants treated with Melatonin are significantly more responsive than Melatonin-treated sibling controls in during all the habituation phases, and are indistinguishable from vehicle-treated sibling controls. 
This is broadly also true for Latency, Bend Amplitude and Movement Duration, where this statistical relationship is observed in most epochs we analyze statistically and always in the "Trained Response" epoch of training blocks 2-4 (\autoref{fig:5}Biv-Div), representing the period where the Melatonin-dependent phenotype is the most robust (\autoref{fig:1}).
These results indicate that the double mutants show a complete loss of sensitivity to the effects of Melatonin on habituation learning, and that this is true for both the components of habituation potentiated by Melatonin (Probability and Latency), and those that are inhibited (Bend Amplitude and Movement Duration).  
From these data we can conclude that Mtnr1aa and Mtnr1al cooperatively mediate the effects of Melatonin on habituation learning.

We envision two models for how Mtnr1aa and Mtnr1al could cooperatively mediate the effects of Melatonin on habituation learning.
In the first model, Mtnr1aa and Mtnr1al could be expressed in the same neurons, and therefore could act in a cooperative manner within the same cells to mediate the effects of Melatonin on habituation learning.
In the second model, Mtnr1aa and Mtnr1al could be expressed in different neurons, and therefore could act in a cooperative manner across different parts of the circuit to mediate the effects of Melatonin on habituation learning.
To distinguish between these models, we first examined the expression pattern of Mtnr1aa and Mtnr1al in the brain using fluorescent in situ hybridization (HCR) for each of these receptors. 
This confirmed that Mtnr1aa is broadly expressed across the brain (\citealp{Hill2026}, \autoref{fig:6}A), including in many brain regions that we have previously implicated in habituation learning using Ca\textsuperscript{2+} imaging \citep{Lamire2023-he}, such as the Tectum, Tegmentum and Cerebellum, as well as a cluster of neurons in the medial hindbrain, which appears to be a hotspot for MtnR-exrpression \citep{Hill2026}.
Previous \emph{in situ} hybridization data for \emph{mtnr1al}  \citep{Hill2026} did not detect a clear signal for Mtnr1al, but did see faint signal in the Tectum. We did observe some very sparse staining in the tectum for \emph{mtnr1al}, but it was unclear if this staining represents background signals, such as vascular staining. Therefore, these experiments were inconclusive, but strong expression of \emph{mtnr1aa} in the tectum, and potential tectal expression of \emph{mtnr1al}, would be consistent with a model in which these receptors act in the same brain areas or even the same neurons to modulate habituation learning.

Since the expression data was inconclusive, we turned to a whole-brain functional mapping approach to try and identify the functional sites of action for these receptors.
We used the pERK-based MAP-mapping method \citep{Randlett2019-fj} which maps  differential activity comparing across different conditions (e.g. Mutant vs. WT). 
We compared Melatonin-treated mutants vs sibling controls after being exposed to a block of 60 dark flashes to identify where in the brain mutants show differential activity. 
This analysis revealed that the most prominent changes in the \emph{mtnr1aa} mutants were in the Tectum and the vicinity of the retinal arborization fields (AFs) which showed stronger activity in the mutants relative to sibling controls (\autoref{fig:6}C, F). This is consistent with a prominent role for the Tectum, and perhaps other primary visual areas like the pretectum, in mediating habituation learning \citep{Lamire2023-he}, and that Mtnr1aa acts in these areas to reinforce these effects. Other areas of differential activity included weak suppression of the Preoptic Area (POA), and some activation of the Torus Semicircularis.


In contrast, \emph{mtnr1al} mutants showed very little modulation of the tectum, indicating that this is likely not the primary site of action for this receptor. 
However, they showed very strong suppression of the preoptic area (POA) (\autoref{fig:6}C, F). 
The POA was also suppressed in the \emph{mtnr1aa} mutants, but to a much lesser extent.
In contrast, the torus longitudinalis (TL) showed little modulation in the \emph{mtnr1aa} mutants, but strong activation in the \emph{mtnr1al} mutants, indicating that this may be a key site of action for Mtnr1al. 
The \emph{mtnr1al} mutants also showed sparse alterations in activity in distributed hindbrain regions.
Therefore, despite the similar behavioural phenotype of \emph{mtnr1aa} and \emph{mtnr1al} mutants, these activity mapping data reveal a largely differential pattern of activity.

To identify how these receptors function cooperatively, we analyzed double mutants for both \emph{mtnr1aa} and \emph{mtnr1al}. 
These double muatnts showed the strongest differential activity pattern, consistent with the stronger behavioural effects observed in these mutants (\autoref{fig:5}) and a cooperative role for these receptors. 
Indeed, the activity map observed for double-mutants appears to be: 
1) a combination of the more localized effects seen in the individual mutants (including the activation of the tectum, retinal AFs, and TL, as well as suppression of the POA suppression), and 
2) additional alterations in activity (including areas of the pallium and subpallium, habenula, and cerebellum) (\autoref{fig:6}E, F). 
Collectively, these data are consistent with a model in which Mtnr1aa and Mtnr1al act in different parts of the circuit to promote habituation learning, with Mtnr1aa acting primarily in the Tectum and other primary visual areas, and Mtnr1al acting primarily in the POA, TL and hindbrain.
When lost collectively, these effects combine to yield a much more robust and widespread alteration in brain activity patterns in response to DF training. 


\section{Discussion}

Here we show that Melatonin modulates multiple distinct components of visual habituation learning through the cooperative and non-redundant action of two MT1-type receptors, Mtnr1aa and Mtnr1al.
Using an expanded behavioral dataset, we demonstrate that Melatonin exerts bidirectional effects on the dark-flash O-bend response, potentiating habituation of response probability and latency while inhibiting habituation of movement bend amplitude and duration.
Systematic CRISPR mutagenesis of all six zebrafish Melatonin receptors identifies Mtnr1aa and Mtnr1al as the sole mediators: individual receptor mutants show partial insensitivity, while double mutants show complete insensitivity.
Whole-brain pERK activity mapping reveals that double mutants show a distributed map of altered activity, and that the individual mutants show alterations in activity that segregate by location, rather than simularly localized but weaker patterns than the double mutants. 
These data indicate that Mtnr1aa acts strongly in the optic tectum and in the vicinity of the retinal arborization fields, while Mtnr1al exerts its influence in the preoptic area (POA) and torus longitudinalis (TL).
Together, these results establish a molecular and circuit-level framework for how a circadianly-regulated neuroendocrine hormone shapes multiple independent habituation streams through receptor paralogs with divergent circuit specificities.

\subsection*{Melatonin bidirectionally modulates distinct habituation mechanisms}

A central conclusion from studies of habituation in larval zebrafish is that this behavior is inherently modular: response probability, latency, amplitude, and duration each habituate through molecularly distinct and largely independent mechanisms operating in parallel across distributed brain circuits \citep{Randlett2019-fj, Nelson2022, Lamire2023-he}.
This is consistent with the principle, established across organisms from \emph{C.\ elegans} to mammals, that habituation is not a unitary synaptic process but emerges from multiple parallel plasticity events at distinct circuit nodes \citep{ramaswami2014network, Cooke2020, McDiarmid2019-td}.
Our data show that Melatonin engages this modular organization selectively and bidirectionally: it accelerates habituation learning for response probability and latency while slowing it for amplitude and duration, leaving displacement and reorientation largely unaffected.
This pattern is fundamentally incompatible with a global arousal-suppressing action — if Melatonin were simply inducing sedation, all response parameters would shift in the same direction, and the acoustic startle response would also be suppressed.
Critically, the acoustic response is indistinguishable between Melatonin-treated and control animals at the concentrations used \citep{Lamire2023-he}, confirming that Melatonin's effects are sensorimotor-circuit-specific rather than a consequence of sleep promotion \citep{Gandhi2015_NEURON, Hill2026}. 
Recently, \citealp{Hill2026} propose that Melatonin acts to suppress visual responses, and that this mechanism of stimulus insensitivity may explain the sleep-promoting effects of Melatonin. 
They found that larvae treated with Melatonin are less sensitive to a weak visual dimming stimulus, but this effect was only reported for the population of fish that had similarly responded to a strong visual dimming stimulus preceeding the weak stimulus. 
Our data are not consistent with this model. 
Instead, we find that the primary effect of Melatonin is the promotion of the habitiation learning process. 
Further Melatonin not only suppresses, but also potentiates aspects of these visual responses. 
While it is clear that Melatonin affects visual responsivness, our analyeses indicate that the role of Melatonin on the visual system is rather more complex than a simple suppression of responsiveness. 

The bidirectional nature of Melatonin's effects on plasticity is not without precedent.
Exogenous Melatonin modulates multiple distinct forms of synaptic plasticity in hippocampal slices in a parameter-dependent manner, attenuating low-frequency evoked population spikes while modifying paired-pulse facilitation and inhibition in opposite directions depending on inter-stimulus interval \citep{El-Sherif2003-vt}.
At the molecular level, Gi-coupled Melatonin receptor signaling converges on cAMP/CREB and downstream kinase pathways whose consequences for synaptic plasticity are highly state- and circuit-dependent \citep{ValdsTovar2018, Jilg2019-oy, MalenkaAndBear2004}.
The specific context of habituation training — where distinct molecular programs independently regulate distinct response components — thus provides a revealing readout through which the bidirectional circuit-specificity of Melatonin can be dissected.

\subsection*{The role of endogenously produced Melatonin in habituation learning} 

That endogenous Melatonin deficiency (\emph{aanat1;aanat2} double mutants) produces a deficit specifically for response probability but not for amplitude or duration likely reflects the fundamental limitations of the visual assay: dark-flash stimulation requires light-adaptation, which is itself a potent suppressor of pineal and retinal Melatonin synthesis.
The more selective deficit of endogenous deficiency compared to the bidirectional effects of pharmacological Melatonin may therefore reflect a physiological ceiling on achievable Melatonin concentrations during the assay, rather than a true mechanistic difference in the circuits that endogenous Melatonin normally modulates.

A notable feature of our endogenous Melatonin experiments is that the habituation deficit requires loss of both Aanat1 and Aanat2, with neither single mutant alone producing a robust phenotype.
This functional redundancy is interesting in light of the distinct anatomical sources of these two enzymes: Aanat2 drives Melatonin synthesis in both the pineal gland and the retina, whereas Aanat1 is expressed almost exclusively in the retina \citep{Gandhi2015_NEURON, Hill2026}.
Previous behavioral studies of endogenous Melatonin in zebrafish have focused primarily on the pineal gland as the relevant source of systemic hormone: \citet{Gandhi2015_NEURON} found that \emph{aanat2} single mutants show disrupted sleep consolidation — consistent with a dominant pineal contribution to circulating nocturnal Melatonin — while \emph{aanat1} single mutants did not have a phenotype in this context.
Similarly, Rawashdeh et al. attributed the night-time suppression of operant memory to pineal-derived Melatonin, based on the effects of pinealectomy \citep{Rawashdeh2007-bw}.
In contrast, \citealt{Hill2026} demonstrated that endogenous Melatonin suppresses visual responsiveness at night through \emph{aanat2}-derived Melatonin, explicitly noting that their \emph{aanat2} mutants retain retinal Melatonin (from Aanat1), and separately confirming that \emph{aanat1} mutants do not worsen the \emph{aanat2} sleep phenotype — establishing pineal-derived Melatonin as the primary relevant source in that context.
Our habituation data suggest a different relationship between these two Melatonin sources: the requirement for loss of \emph{both} Aanat1 and Aanat2 implies that neither source alone is sufficient to sustain normal habituation.
Importantly, however, this redundancy may reflect two \emph{retinal} Melatonin sources rather than a retinal–pineal dichotomy.
Since Aanat2 is expressed in the retina as well as the pineal gland, the functional overlap we observe may be between Aanat1-derived and Aanat2-derived retinal Melatonin, with the pineal contributing little in our assay conditions.
Consistent with this interpretation, our assay requires light-adaptation, which robustly suppresses pineal Melatonin synthesis, and we observed no circadian dependence of the habituation deficit — day-tested (\autoref{fig:2}A) and night-tested (\autoref{fig:2}G) double mutants showed similarly impaired habituation, the opposite of what would be expected if pineal-derived Melatonin (which is strongly elevated only at night) were the primary relevant source.
Together, these observations raise the possibility that retinal Melatonin — produced locally in a light-regulated, cell-autonomous manner — is the dominant endogenous source modulating habituation in this context, with the two retinal AANAT paralogs providing redundant synthetic capacity.
Further work, including retina-specific rescue experiments and measurements of local retinal versus circulating Melatonin levels during the assay, will be required to test this hypothesis directly.

An equally open question is how retinal Melatonin reaches the brain circuits where it acts.
Retinal Melatonin is generally considered a local neuromodulator, acting in to regulate photoreceptor function and retinal signal processing,\citep{Besharse2016, Bhoi2023}, and therefore it could exert its influence on habituation via the modulation retinal ganglion cell activity.
However, Melatonin is a small (\textasciitilde232 Da), highly lipophilic molecule that crosses membranes by passive diffusion.
Melatonin produced in the retina could conceivably diffuse inward through the retina, and along the optic nerve towards central targets.
Strikingly, the most prominent site of differential pERK activity in \emph{mtnr1aa} mutants are proximal to the termination zones of retinal axons in the tectum — which are in immediate anatomical proximity to the retinal source and represent the first central synaptic station for retinal output.
Whether Mtnr1aa at this location is activated by retinal Melatonin arriving by diffusion, by systemic Melatonin, or by Melatonin modulating the retinal output signal itself remains to be determined, but the anatomical coincidence between the site of MtnR action and the termination of retinal projections makes the retina a compelling candidate source.

\subsection*{Functional specialization of two cooperative MT1-type receptors}

Of the six zebrafish Melatonin receptors spanning both MT1-type and MT2-type clades, only the two MT1-type paralogs Mtnr1aa and Mtnr1al contribute to Melatonin's effects on habituation.
The dispensability of MT2-type receptors is notable.
In mammals, MT2 is required for normal hippocampal long-term potentiation \citep{Larson2006} and long-term recognition memory \citep{Pistono2021}, and deletion of either MT1 or MT2 alone is sufficient to abrogate methamphetamine-induced conditioned place preference \citep{Clough2014}, demonstrating functional roles for both subtypes in plasticity.
Therefore, it is clear that MT2-type receptors are able to influence plasticity in some contexts. While this may highlight a difference between zebrafish and mammalian MT2-type receptors, and more likely explanation for their dispensability in our assay is that they may simply not expressed in the relevant circuits implementing habituation. 
Differential expression may also explain the dispensability of the MT1-type receptor Mtnr1ab, as well as Mtnr1c, which is homologous to the Melatonin-insensitive GPR50 receptor in mammals and may not even be a functional Melatonin receptor.


The cooperative non-redundancy of Mtnr1aa and Mtnr1al adds a further layer of specialization: each single mutant loses approximately half of the Melatonin-dependent habituation enhancement across all response components, and double mutants lose it entirely.
This additive, non-redundant relationship is the behavioral signature of two receptors mediating independent, non-overlapping arms of the same modulatory signal — a conclusion the pERK mapping data confirm at the circuit level.

\subsection*{Mtnr1aa and Mtnr1al act in non-overlapping brain circuits}

Our whole-brain pERK activity mapping revealed an anatomical dissociation between the functional footprints of the two receptors.
In Melatonin-treated \emph{mtnr1aa} mutants, the optic tectum and retinal arborization fields show the most prominent increases in activity after dark-flash training relative to sibling controls \citep{Randlett2019-fj, Lamire2023-he}.
In striking contrast, \emph{mtnr1al} mutants show minimal tectal modulation but instead display prominent suppression of the preoptic area (POA) and activation of the torus longitudinalis (TL).
The POA is a conserved vertebrate hub for sleep and arousal, where galanin-expressing neurons integrate sleep pressure \citep{Reichert2019-sz} and MT1/MT2 receptors have been proposed to gate sleep onset \citep{Ng2017, Klosen2019}; in our context, Mtnr1al appears to sustain POA activity during training.
The TL is a ray-finned fish-specific midbrain structure that provides tonic glutamatergic input to apical dendrites of tectal pyramidal neurons \citep{Folgueira2020-tl, DeMarco2021}, is required for binocularity and spatial summation of visual responses in the tectum \citep{Tesmer2022}, and participates in a dimming-selective feedback pathway from the tectum \citep{Robles2021} engaged by our dark-flash stimulus.
The double mutants show a combined and amplified version of both individual activity maps — encompassing the tectum, retinal AFs, POA, TL, and additional distributed changes in the pallium, subpallium, habenula, and cerebellum — consistent with cooperative control of parallel arms of the habituation circuit.

An important caveat is that differential pERK identifies where neural activity is altered in the mutants, not necessarily where the receptors act directly.
The strongest pERK changes could reflect secondary consequences of receptor action upstream: for example, elevated tectal pERK in \emph{mtnr1aa} mutants could result from Mtnr1aa acting on tectal neurons, but equally from Mtnr1aa acting on presynaptic inputs to the tectum — including retinal ganglion cell terminals in the arborization fields — and thereby modulating the drive those inputs exert.
Similarly, whether the POA and TL changes in \emph{mtnr1al} mutants reflect independent primary sites of receptor action, or whether one is a downstream consequence of the other, cannot be resolved from activity maps alone.
Cell-type-specific expression profiling and targeted perturbations will be required to identify the direct cellular substrates of each receptor and disentangle these possibilities.
While these experiments can not conclusively localize the site of action of the receptors, the anatomical segregation of the pERK changes in the two mutants strongly suggests that Mtnr1aa and Mtnr1al act in different parts of the circuit to promote habituation learning.

\subsection*{Broader significance}

These results provide a receptor-resolved and circuit-level account of how a neuroendocrine hormone shapes a fundamental form of learning.
The identification of two MT1-type paralogs with non-overlapping circuit specificities demonstrates that the functional diversity of Melatonin's actions on habituation does not arise from a single brain region or a single signaling mechanism, but from the coordinated recruitment of multiple distinct neural structures.
This architecture implies that the evolutionary expansion of the Melatonin receptor repertoire in teleost fishes \citep{Hill2026, Falcn2009} provides a substrate for parcellating Melatonin's modulatory influence across circuits with greater precision than would be achievable through a single broadly expressed receptor, achieving behavioral specificity at the receptor-expression level rather than solely at the signaling level.

Deficits in habituation are a consistent, cross-diagnostic feature of neurodevelopmental and psychiatric conditions including autism spectrum disorder, schizophrenia, and ADHD \citep{McDiarmid2017}, and altered circadian rhythms and Melatonin profiles are frequently documented in these populations; aberrant MT1-type Melatonin receptor signaling could therefore contribute to habituation impairments in these disorders.
The clinically available MT1/MT2 agonists ramelteon and agomelatine are prescribed for insomnia and depression, respectively \citep{Liu2016}; understanding how these drugs modulate the habituation circuits identified here is relevant to their broader cognitive effects.
More fundamentally, the engagement of POA sleep-homeostasis circuitry in the modulation of non-associative habituation learning raises the intriguing possibility that sleep-regulatory machinery is mechanistically intertwined with sensorimotor plasticity, and that Melatonin serves as a coordinating hormonal signal that couples the brain's learning state to the circadian regulation of arousal.
Future experiments combining cell-type-specific imaging and chemogenetic or optogenetic perturbations of POA, TL, and tectal neurons in animals expressing or lacking Mtnr1aa or Mtnr1al will be required to resolve the synaptic mechanisms through which these receptors independently modulate the multiple plasticity streams that together constitute visual habituation learning in the larval zebrafish.


\section{Materials and Methods}

\subsection{Animal husbandry and care}
All experiments were carried out using larval zebrafish between 5 and 6 days post-fertilization (dpf), maintained in E3 embryo medium supplemented with 0.02\% HEPES (pH 7.2) at a density of approximately 1 larva per mL.
Larvae were housed in 10 cm petri dishes under a 14:10 h light/dark cycle (lights-on at 08:00, lights-off at 20:00) at a constant temperature of 28-29°C.
Zebrafish were maintained and bred at two facilities: the Plateau de Recherche Expérimentale en Criblage In Vivo (PRECI, SFR Biosciences, Lyon) and the Animalerie Zebrafish Rockefeller (AZR, SFR Santé Lyon Est, Lyon).
Adult zebrafish husbandry was carried out in compliance with the regulations of the PRECI and AZR facilities, under the oversight of the regional ethics committee (comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale de la Région Rhône-Alpes: CECCAPP, Agreement \#C693870602).
Larval behavioral experiments were conducted at 5 dpf and are therefore not subject to ethical review under French regulations, as the larvae are not yet considered protected animals at this stage.
Mutants for the Melatonin-synthesizing arylalkylamine N-acetyltransferase genes (\emph{aanat1}, \emph{aanat2}) were a gift from Dr David Prober, and are as described in \citep{Gandhi2015_NEURON}.


\subsection{Generation of CRISPR mutant lines}
CRISPR-Cas9 mutant lines targeting all six zebrafish Melatonin receptor genes (\emph{mtnr1aa}, \emph{mtnr1ab}, \emph{mtnr1al}, \emph{mtnr1ba}, \emph{mtnr1bb}, \emph{mtnr1c}) were generated using standard zebrafish CRISPR injection protocols in the TLF  background (ZDB-GENO-990623-2).
Guide RNAs (gRNAs) were designed to target early coding exons of each gene using Millipore CRISPR Design Tools (\url{https://www.milliporesigmabioinfo.com/bioinfo_tools/faces/secured/crispr/crispr.xhtml}), and were synthesized by Sigma.
Ribonucleoprotein complexes were assembled by combining gRNA with recombinant Cas9 protein (Sigma) and injected into the cell of one-cell-stage embryos.
Founders (F0) were outcrossed to wild-type fish, and heterozygous F1 carriers were identified by PCR followed by Sanger sequencing of the target locus from fin-clip genomic DNA.
For each gene, an allele bearing a frameshift-inducing insertion or deletion in the target area was selected and propagated.
gRNA sequences and resulting indel mutations for each line are provided in Supplementary Table~\ref{supptab:primers}.
Lines were maintained as heterozygous stocks.
For behavioral experiments, clutches were generated from heterozygous incrosses and sorted by genotyping as described below; homozygous mutants were compared with pooled heterozygous and wild-type siblings.
The \emph{mtnr1aa\textsubscript{-/-}};\emph{mtnr1al\textsubscript{-/-}} double-mutant line was generated by intercrossing the double-mutant heterozygous line \emph{mtnr1aa\textsubscript{+/-}};\emph{mtnr1al\textsubscript{+/-}}.

\subsection{Genotyping}
Larvae were individually lysed in 50 mM NaOH at 95\degree C for 10 min and neutralized with 1 M Tris-HCl (pH 8.0) at a 1:9 ratio (v/v) to extract genomic DNA.
Target loci were amplified using FIREPol\textregistered{} Master Mix Ready to Load 1$\times$ (Solis Biodyne) with gene-specific primers at 0.1 \textmu M.
Alleles with resolvable size differences were distinguished by fragment analysis on high-density (2.5--3\%) agarose gels.
Primer sequences and expected amplicon sizes for each mutant line are provided in Supplementary Table~\ref{supptab:primers}.


\subsection{Behavioural testing and drug treatments}
Melatonin (Sigma-Aldrich, M5250) was dissolved in DMSO to a 1 mM (1000x) stock solution and stored at $-$20\degree C.
Working solutions were prepared fresh before each experiment by a 1:100 dilution into E3 yielding a \SI{1}{\micro\Molar} (10x) Melatonin stock solution in 1\% DMSO. 
Larvae were distributed into the 300-well plates with one larva per well in \SI{225}{\micro\litre} of E3 using a p1000 pipette tip with the end cut off to allow for gentle transfer.
\SI{25}{\micro\litre} of the \SI{1}{\micro\Molar} Melatonin stock was then added directly into the wells of the 300-well plate containing larvae, yielding a final concentration of \SI{1}{\micro\Molar} Melatonin in 0.01\% DMSO. 
Vehicle controls consisted of E3 containing 0.1\% DMSO, via the same dilution procedure.
Experiments were generally started in the morning/early afternoon, between 11:00-13:00.
For experiments using \emph{aanat} double mutants, larvae were maintained in drug-free E3 throughout.
To test Melatonin responses at circadian night, \emph{aanat1\textsuperscript{-/-};aanat2\textsuperscript{-/-}} larvae were raised under a reversed 14:10 light/dark cycle such that lights-off coincided with the start of the experimental period (11:00);  behavioral testing was performed during this reversed dark phase, approximately at ZT12 of the reversed cycle.
For these \emph{aanat} mutant experiments, larvae were loaded into the plate at 4dpf and kept in the incubator overnight until testing at 5dpf, to minimize handling and light exposure on the testing day. 


\subsection{Analyses of the Dark Flash Response}
Larval behavior was assessed in custom 300-well acrylic plates (8 mm diameter, 6 mm depth; ~300 µL water volume) as previously described \citep{Hsiao2025}.
The plates were positioned beneath a 31\degree C water bath, which served as a heated lid to prevent condensation and maintain the wells at 29\degree C.
Recordings were captured using a Mikrotron CXP-4 camera (500 Hz) connected to a Silicon Software frame grabber (Marathon ACX-QP, Basler) and illuminated with IR LEDs (TSHF5410, digikey.com).
Visual stimulation was provided by a rectangular array of 155 WS2813 RGB LEDs, with dark flashes (DFs) consisting of a 1 second light-off period followed by a linear return to baseline over 19 seconds.
Acoustic/vibration taps were delivered via a solenoid (ROB-10391, Sparkfun).
The experimental design alternated 1 hour of stimulation with 1 hour of rest, during which a stepper motor-driven actuator (Hanpose HPV4, 500 cm) moved the camera between two plates, enabling up to 600 larvae to be tested simultaneously.

Control of the LEDs, solenoid, and camera actuator was managed by a Raspberry Pi Pico running CircuitPython and custom Python software, which also handled video acquisition and online tracking of head and tail positions at 20-30 Hz.
During DF or tap delivery, a 1 second high-speed burst recording was captured and later analyzed offline using background subtraction and morphological operations.
Tracking and analysis relied on open-source libraries, including OpenCV, scikit-image, NumPy, SciPy, and Numba.

\subsection{Fluorescent \emph{in situ} hybridization (HCR)}
To map the expression of \emph{mtnr1aa} and \emph{mtnr1al} in the larval brain, we performed fluorescent \emph{in situ} hybridization (HCR v3.0) using probes designed by Molecular Instruments (Los Angeles, CA), using their standard protocol \citep{Choi2018}.
Stained brains were mounted in 2\% LMP agarose () and imaged from the dorsal suface on a Zeiss 880 confocal microscope using a 10\texttimes{} air objective with NA = 0.45 (CIQLE imaging platform, Lyon France).


\subsection{MAP-Mapping Analyses of Brain-Wide Activity Patterns}
Whole-brain pERK activity mapping was performed using the MAP-mapping approach \citep{Randlett2019-fj}.
Larvae (5dpf) from crosses of heterozygous Melatonin receptor double mutants were treated with \SI{1}{\micro\Molar} Melatonin and .
Approximately one hour after treatment onset, larvae were transferred in groups to 10 cm petri dishes in the dark flash behavior setup and allowed to acclimatize for 20 minutes.
Larvae were then subjected to a block of 60 dark flashes (1 second light-off, 1 minute inter-stimulus interval) prior to immediate fixation in 4\% paraformaldehyde in PBS.
Control (na\"ive) cohorts were transferred to the same setup and acclimatized for the equivalent duration without receiving dark flashes before fixation.
Fixed larvae were processed for immunohistochemistry using anti-phospho-p44/42 MAPK (ERK1/2) antibody for pERK (Cell Signaling Technology, \#4370S) and anti-p44/42 MAPK (ERK1/2) antibody for total ERK (tERK; Cell Signaling Technology, \#4696S), followed by fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies: Alexa Fluor\texttrademark{} 647 for tERK (Thermo Fisher Scientific, \#A21235) and Alexa Fluor\texttrademark{} 568 for pERK (Thermo Fisher Scientific, \#11036).
Brains were imaged on a Zeiss 880 confocal microscope using a 10\texttimes{} air objective with NA = 0.45 (CIQLE imaging platform, Lyon France).
Image stacks were acquired at a voxel resolution of 0.08 \texttimes{} 0.08 \texttimes{} 2 \textmu m, and two partially overlapping volumes were tiled to encompass the entire brain.
The tERK channel was used as an anatomical reference for nonlinear registration of each specimen to a standardized larval zebrafish reference brain via the Computational Morphometry Toolkit (CMTK).
Registered image volumes were subsequently down-sampled to 300 \texttimes{} 679 \texttimes{} 80 voxels (x, y, z) and spatially smoothed using a two-dimensional Gaussian filter.
To account for inter-individual variability in staining intensity, voxel-wise pERK signals were normalized to corresponding tERK values prior to statistical comparison across experimental groups.
Each mutant genotype was analyzed independently, comparing homozygous mutant larvae to pooled sibling controls (heterozygotes and wild-type) within the same Melatonin treatment condition.
Genotypes were confirmed post-hoc from tissue collected after brain imaging. 
These analyses were performed using custom Python scripts, available at \href{https://github.com/owenrandlett/2026_MtnRs_Hab/tree/main/code/MAPMapping}{github.com/owenrandlett/2026\_MtnRs\_Hab/code/MAPMapping}.


\subsection{Data analysis and statistics}
All behavioral data analyses were performed using custom Python scripts (NumPy, SciPy, Pandas, Matplotlib, Seaborn, scikit-posthocs), available at \href{https://github.com/owenrandlett/2026_MtnRs_Hab/tree/main/code}{github.com/owenrandlett/2026\_MtnRs\_Hab/code}.
Per-fish group-average response traces across the habituation session were smoothed using a Savitzky-Golay filter (window = 15 stimuli, polynomial order = 2) for visualization.
For statistical analysis, responses were averaged within each defined epoch per fish.
Epoch means were plotted as individual data points (jittered strip plot) overlaid on violin plots; the violin shape represents the kernel density estimate of the per-fish epoch means, using a Gaussian kernel with Scott's bandwidth rule and restricted to the range of the data (\texttt{cut=0}).
Group means are indicated by white circles.
Statistical comparisons between two groups were performed using the Mann-Whitney U test.
For comparisons among three or more groups, the Kruskal-Wallis H-test was used, followed by Dunn's post-hoc pairwise comparisons with Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons, as implemented in the scikit-posthocs library \citep{Terpilowski2019}.
